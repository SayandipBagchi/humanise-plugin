# Pattern catalogue

Twenty-one named patterns, grouped into the five families in `SKILL.md`. Read this during step 1 of a rewrite.

A **strong** tell justifies an edit on one sighting. A tell marked ***weak alone*** needs company from other patterns in the same passage, because a careful writer makes any one of these choices on purpose.

## A. Staging instead of stating

The strongest and most frequent family. Act on one sighting.

### 1. Not X but Y

**Watch for:** not X but Y; not just, not only, or not merely X, but Y; "it's not X, it's Y"; the reversed form X rather than Y; the same contrast split across two sentences; a clipped negative tail such as ", no guessing." Treat equivalent constructions in any language the same way.

**Problem:** The negative half names something no one claimed, so the positive half sounds larger without adding a claim. State the point directly. Keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects a belief the reader actually holds, or when both halves carry information.

**Before:** "It's not just about the beat; it's part of the aggression and atmosphere. It's not merely a song, it's a statement."

**After:** "The heavy beat adds to the aggressive tone."

### 2. Restating instead of continuing

**Watch for:** a one-sentence paragraph that restates the paragraph before it; "That is the real win"; "Read that again"; "Let that sink in"; the same closer after several sections; a row of fragments; one word in all caps or with periods between words. Also the opening form: a heading followed by a one-line paragraph that repeats the heading before the real content starts.

**Problem:** The line asks the reader to pause on a claim instead of adding to it, whether it sits before the content or after it. Cut a closer that repeats. Cut a first sentence that only echoes its heading and let the first informative sentence lead. Merge fragments into a sentence with a specific claim.

**Before:** "Then the new system arrived. No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia. The old rules were gone."

**After:** "The new system changed the search because it did not favour human-looking designs. That made some older assumptions less useful."

### 3. Sayings that sound deep

**Watch for:** "the real question is," "at its core," "what really matters," "the deeper issue," "the heart of the matter," "X is the Y of Z," "X becomes a trap," "the language of," "the currency of," "the architecture of."

**Problem:** An ordinary point is dressed as a hidden truth or an aphorism. Replace the saying with the specific claim.

**Before:** "The real question is whether teams can adapt. At its core, what really matters is organisational readiness."

**After:** "The question is whether teams can adapt. That depends on whether the organisation is ready to change its habits."

### 4. Staged run-up before the point

**Watch for:** "Let's dive in," "let's explore," "let's break this down," "here's what you need to know," "now let's look at," "without further ado," "heads up," "quick note," "honestly?" "look," "here's the thing," "the thing is," "let's be honest," and staged casual openings such as "one thing that bit me, so pay attention."

**Problem:** The writer announces the point, or stages a moment of candour, instead of making the point. Remove the run-up, not just its tone. A word such as "honestly" inside a genuine casual sentence can stay.

**Before:** "Let's dive into how caching works. Here's what you need to know."

**After:** "The application caches data at multiple layers."

### 5. Arguing with no one

**Watch for:** "This isn't mainly about," "I'm not saying," "to be clear," "don't get me wrong," "this is not to say," "some might say," "a tempting approach would be," "one might be tempted to," "you might think... but," "it would be easy to just."

**Problem:** The text answers an objection or rejects an option that appears nowhere else, usually a leftover from an earlier draft. Remove the defence. If it carries a real claim, state that claim. Keep an objection the text attributes and answers in full, and keep an option a reader would actually weigh.

## B. Rhythm by rule

A writer may use any one of these on purpose, so the weaker ones need company.

### 6. Forced triads

**Problem:** Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete, whether the meaning has three parts or not. The tell can be one sentence, three parallel examples, or three short facts followed by a lesson. Check that each item adds a distinct idea. Merge examples, develop the strongest one, or vary the structure when they do not. Keep three real items when the meaning needs three.

### 7. Repeated sentence openings

**Problem:** Several sentences in a row start with the same subject, because repetition is handled by rule instead of by ear. Merge the sentences, change the subject, or begin with the action. Do not ban the repeated word: deliberate repetition carries rhythm.

### 8. Dashes as the universal connector

**Rule:** The final rewrite contains no em dashes or en dashes unless the user's writing sample uses them, in which case match the sample's rate. This includes spaced dashes and double hyphens used as dashes. Replace each with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses, or rewrite the sentence. Leave dashes and hyphens inside code blocks, inline code, commands, paths, and URLs alone.

**Problem:** A dash lets the writer skip choosing how two clauses relate, so a model reaches for it everywhere. As *evidence* that a passage was generated, a single dash proves nothing; careful writers use dashes deliberately. The output rule above is separate and stays absolute: read a lone dash as weak evidence about the source, and still remove it from the rewrite.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers

**Watch for:** "to be fair," "it's also possible," "could potentially," "might arguably," "in some cases it may," and repeated statements such as "this is an inference."

**Problem:** Repeated editing adds one qualifier after another until every claim sounds uncertain. Keep a qualifier only when the text supports it and the meaning needs it. Keep scope statements, legal and safety notices, and real corrections. ***Weak alone.***

### 10. Hyphenated pairs everywhere

**Watch for:** third-party, cross-functional, client-facing, data-driven, decision-making, well-known, high-quality, real-time, long-term, end-to-end.

**Problem:** These pairs get hyphenated in every position. Keep the hyphen before a noun where grammar needs it, as in "a high-quality report"; drop it after the noun, as in "the report is high quality." ***Weak alone.***

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually sound. Keep the fact and remove the dressing.

### 11. Overused model words

**Watch for:** actually, additionally, align with, bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasising, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, figurative gate or gating, highlight as a verb, interplay, intricate or intricacies, key as an adjective, landscape as an abstract noun, meticulous or meticulously, pivotal, quietly, figurative robust, showcase, tapestry as an abstract noun, testament, underscore as a verb, valuable, vibrant.

**Problem:** Models reach for these too often, especially in groups. This is the shared word list for this family; patterns 12 and 14 rely on it rather than repeating it. It is a watch list, not a blacklist. A formal word outside it is not a tell by itself. Keep technical uses such as a feature gate or a robust guarantee.

### 12. Inflated significance

**Watch for:** "stands as a testament," "a pivotal moment," "plays a key role," "marking or shaping the," "underscores its importance," "reflects a broader legacy," "setting the stage for," "evolving landscape," "indelible mark," stock "challenges and outlook" sections, and send-offs such as "the future looks bright."

Also the **-ing rider**, the compressed form of the same move: an *-ing* phrase bolted onto a plain fact to make it sound deeper. Highlighting, underscoring, emphasising, ensuring, reflecting, symbolising, contributing to, cultivating, fostering, encompassing, showcasing.

**Problem:** An ordinary detail is said to mark a change, prove a legacy, or promise a future. Keep the fact and drop the significance. Keep an *-ing* rider only when the text actually supports what it claims. End on the last concrete fact unless the text gives real plans.

### 13. Sales language

**Watch for:** boasts, rich in a figurative sense, profound, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, figurative groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array, breathtaking, must-visit, stunning. Plus the promotional half of the pattern 11 list.

**Problem:** The text reads like an advertisement, usually for a place, product, culture, or organisation. State what the thing is.

### 14. Vague or evasive verbs

**Watch for two forms.** *Vague relationship:* associated with, in association with, connected to, in connection with, linked to, tied to. *Avoided plain verb:* serves as, stands as, functions as, operates as, marks, represents, boasts, features, offers, maintains, refers to, where a simple verb works.

**Problem:** Either the verb does not say what the relationship is, or a longer phrase stands in for a plain one. Name the relationship the text actually gives, and use *is*, *are*, and *has* when they carry the meaning. If the text does not give a relationship, keep the vague wording rather than inventing a role.

### 15. Borrowed authority

**Watch for:** experts argue, observers have cited, industry reports, some critics, several publications, cited or featured in a list of prestige outlets, "active social media presence," follower counts.

**Problem:** An unnamed authority props up a claim, or a list of outlets props up a person. Where the text names the real source and what it said, use that. Otherwise cut the unsupported claim or list. Never invent a source. A missing citation alone is not a tell; most writing is unsourced.

### 16. Passive voice and missing subjects

**Problem:** The text hides who acts, or drops the subject. Use active voice when it makes the actor and the action clearer. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, unimportant, deliberately withheld, or when the affected thing belongs first. ***Weak alone.***

## D. Formatting by rule

The tell is decoration on *every* item, not any one formatting choice.

### 17. Bold as decoration

**Problem:** Words are bolded without a reason, and vertical lists give every item a bold label and a colon. Remove the bold. Turn a labelled list into prose when the labels carry no information of their own. Keep bold where it has a real navigation or emphasis purpose.

### 18. Decorative headings and typography

**Problem:** Headings capitalise every main word; headings and list items carry emojis or arrows as decoration; a horizontal rule sits between every section; a document opens with a heading that repeats its own title. Curly quotation marks appear where the writer or the target format uses straight ones.

Use sentence case, remove decoration and unnecessary rules, let the title stand once, and match the user's quote convention. The typography half is ***weak alone***: preserve quotes that belong to a deliberate style or an exact quotation.

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

Remove these outright. They do not need a rewrite.

### 19. Chatbot residue

**Watch for:** "I hope this helps," "Of course," "Certainly," "Great question," "You're absolutely right," "Would you like...?", "Want me to...?", "Should I continue?", "let me know," "here is a..."

**Problem:** A greeting, praise, offer, or closing remains in text that should stand on its own. Remove the wrapper and keep the content.

### 20. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses

**Watch for:** "as of [date]," "up to my last training update," "specific details are limited," "based on available information," "not publicly available," "not widely documented," "in the provided sources," "maintains a low profile," "likely grew up," "it is believed that."

**Problem:** The text mentions where the writer's knowledge ends, or admits it found no detail and then fills the gap with a plausible guess. State what the supplied material does not show, or remove the sentence. Never present a guess as a fact.

### 21. Writing about the previous version

**Problem:** Documentation and comments describe what the text replaced instead of the current behaviour. Mention a previous version only in a change log, release note, migration guide, or other document about change. Describe what the thing does now.

## When not to act

Each pattern describes a default choice, and a person can make any one of them on purpose. Act on a ***weak alone*** tell only when several tells share a passage. Leave a watched phrase alone inside a quotation, title, proper name, or a passage that discusses the phrase rather than uses it.

Do not infer authorship from a pattern audit. Several tells can justify an edit; style alone cannot prove who wrote a passage.

Keep details that carry the writer's voice unless they damage the meaning:

- A specific, unusual detail.
- Mixed feelings and unresolved tension.
- Dated references, slang, memes, or in-jokes tied to a particular time or group.
- A first-person choice the writer can explain.
- A genuine aside, parenthetical, or self-correction.
